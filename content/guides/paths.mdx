If nobody in your family works in medicine, the letters are genuinely
confusing. People use "doctor," "provider," and "clinician" as if they were
interchangeable. They are not, and the differences decide roughly a decade of
your life.

## The short version

**MD and DO** are both physicians. Both take four years of medical school
after a bachelor's degree, both require residency training afterward, and both
lead to a full, unrestricted medical license. DO programs additionally teach
osteopathic manipulative medicine. **A DO is not a lesser MD** — they are two
routes to the same job, and Texas has two DO schools.

**PA** is a physician assistant. Master's-level training, typically about two
to three years after a bachelor's degree, then you practice as part of a
physician-led team.

**NP** is a nurse practitioner. You become a registered nurse first, then earn
a graduate nursing degree.

**RN** is a registered nurse. The fastest route into direct patient care —
via an associate degree or a bachelor's in nursing.

## Time, honestly

Becoming a physician takes roughly **11 to 16 years** after high school: four
years of college, four years of medical school, and three to eight more years
of residency and fellowship depending on specialty. <Source id="ama-residency" />

Residency is not school. It is a full-time job of 60 to 80 hours a week,
supervised, and paid — modestly. <Source id="ama-residency" />

Between medical school rotations and residency, physicians accumulate roughly
12,000 to 16,000 hours of patient-care training. Nurse practitioner programs
involve on the order of 500 to 750 patient-care hours, and do not require
residency. <Source id="ama-np" />

That difference in hours is the honest core of the comparison. It is not a
statement about who is smarter or who cares more. It is a statement about how
much time each path spends training before independent practice, and it maps
directly onto how long you will be in school, how much debt you will carry,
and how much autonomy you will have at the end.

## How to actually choose

<Callout variant="opinion" title="Judgment call">
  Most people asking "MD or PA?" are really asking one of two other
  questions: *how many more years can I afford to be poor*, or *am I good
  enough to get into medical school*. Those are different problems.

  The first is a real constraint and deserves a real answer — talk to people
  in both roles about money and time. The second is usually a bad reason to
  choose a shorter path. If you want to be a physician and you pick PA
  because you doubt yourself, you will likely spend years wondering. Doubt
  is not data.
</Callout>

Questions worth more than a pros-and-cons list:

- **Do you want to be the one who decides?** Physicians carry final
  responsibility for diagnosis and treatment plans. Some people want that.
  Some very much do not, and that is a legitimate, informed preference.
- **How do you feel about a decade of delayed income?** Not "can you tolerate
  it" in the abstract — look at actual numbers with your actual obligations.
- **Have you watched each job being done?** A day shadowing a PA and a day
  shadowing a physician will teach you more than a month of reading. If you
  cannot arrange shadowing, that itself is information about your access, not
  your ability — and it is a solvable problem.
