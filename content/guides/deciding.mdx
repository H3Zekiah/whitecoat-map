Most advice skips this part and goes straight to grades. That is backwards.
Medicine is a decade of training, a great deal of money, and a specific daily
job that some people love and others find unbearable. Finding out which you
are is the first task, and it is cheaper than any other step.

## The only reliable test

**Go be near the work.** Not near the idea of the work — near the actual job,
on an actual day.

Shadow a physician. Volunteer somewhere with patients rather than paperwork.
Work as a scribe, an EMT, a nursing assistant, a transporter. Anything that
puts you in the room.

What you are checking for is not whether you like helping people. Almost
everyone likes helping people. You are checking how you feel about the parts
nobody advertises: the documentation, the waiting, the families who are
frightened and angry, the patients who do not get better, and the ordinary
grind of a Tuesday.

<Callout variant="opinion" title="Judgment call">
  If you cannot arrange shadowing because you know nobody in medicine, that
  is a fact about your access, not your suitability. It is also solvable —
  hospital volunteer offices, community clinics, and paid entry-level
  clinical jobs do not require connections. Start with a paid one if money is
  tight; it counts just as much.
</Callout>

## Questions that are actually useful

- **Can you be around suffering without either shutting down or falling
  apart?** Not "are you tough" — can you stay present and still function.
- **Do you like being wrong in public?** Medicine is a long apprenticeship of
  being corrected by people senior to you.
- **How do you feel about a decade of delayed income?** Look at real numbers.
- **Is there something else you would rather do?** If a clear answer comes to
  mind, that is worth sitting with.

## Reasons that will not hold

Some motivations are real but insufficient on their own. Wanting to help
people is real — and available in many careers. Wanting the income is real,
and will not survive a bad night in year seven. Wanting to prove something to
family, or to yourself, is extremely common and worth knowing about yourself
before it drives ten years of decisions.

None of these disqualify you. Almost everyone has a mix. Just know which of
yours are load-bearing.

## What if you are not sure

Being unsure is the normal state, and the answer is almost never "decide
harder." It is "get more information cheaply." Take a prerequisite course and
see how it feels. Get a clinical job for a year. Talk to three physicians and
ask what they would tell their younger selves.

<Callout variant="note" title="You are allowed to change your mind">
  Deciding against medicine after real exposure is a successful outcome of
  this process, not a failure of it. Finding out at twenty costs you a
  semester. Finding out at thirty, mid-residency, costs considerably more.
</Callout>
