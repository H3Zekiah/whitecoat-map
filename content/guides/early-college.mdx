If you are in your first or second year of college, you are at the single
most useful moment to be reading this site. Almost everything is still
available to you, including one thing that will not be available next year.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Do this first">
  **JAMP applications are for first- and second-year college students only.**
  If you are economically disadvantaged and a Texas resident, this program
  offers scholarships, paid summer internships, MCAT preparation, and
  guaranteed medical school admission. Apply no later than your second year.
  <Source id="jamp-criteria" /> Read [the JAMP page](/texas/jamp) today, not
  eventually.
</Callout>

## Community college is not a problem

Let us deal with this directly, because it stops people. Starting at a
community college does not disqualify you from medical school. Plenty of
physicians started there, including for the obvious reason that it is
affordable.

What matters is that you eventually demonstrate you can handle upper-level
science coursework at a four-year institution. Some medical schools prefer to
see prerequisite science courses taken at a four-year school. So take your
prerequisites seriously, and when you transfer, take upper-level sciences and
do well in them.

## What actually matters now

**Your science GPA.** Medical schools look at your overall GPA and your
science GPA separately. The science one is the harder one to repair later,
because it only moves when you take more science.

**Building the habit of asking.** Office hours, advisors, professors doing
research. If nobody in your family has done this, the hardest part is
believing you are allowed to knock on the door. You are. That is literally
what those hours are for.

**Finding one clinical experience.** Volunteering at a hospital, working as a
scribe, becoming an EMT or a CNA. One real thing beats five decorative ones.

## What to do this semester

- Check whether your school participates in JAMP and find the campus faculty
  director.
- Map your prerequisite courses across the remaining semesters, working
  backward from when you plan to apply.
- Get one clinical or service commitment started and keep it for more than a
  semester. Duration is what makes it meaningful.
- Introduce yourself to your pre-health advisor if you have one. If your
  school does not have one, that is exactly the gap this site exists to fill.

## What can safely wait

- The MCAT. Do not start studying in your first year.
- Research, unless you are curious about it for its own sake.
- Your school list. It changes anyway.
- Deciding your specialty. Nobody expects this and it will change.

## Common mistakes

- **Overloading on science courses to look impressive** and damaging your GPA
  in the process. A lighter schedule with strong grades beats a heavy one
  with mediocre grades.
- **Collecting activities instead of committing to them.** Admissions
  committees read a year of consistent involvement very differently from six
  one-off events.
- **Waiting until junior year to learn how the process works.** By then
  several doors, JAMP included, have closed.

<Callout variant="opinion" title="Judgment call">
  If you are working twenty hours a week to pay tuition, that is not a hole
  in your application. It is part of your application. Do not let anyone
  convince you that unpaid prestige activities are the only currency —
  though do make sure your application actually says how much you worked,
  because admissions readers cannot infer what you leave out.
</Callout>

## Texas specifics

- Confirm you meet the **Texas residency** requirements and understand what
  keeps them intact. See [Texas residency](/texas/residency).
- Texas has its own application system with an earlier timeline than the rest
  of the country. See [how the Texas application works](/texas/tmdsas).
