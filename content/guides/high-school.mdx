Nothing you do in high school gets you into medical school. Medical schools
do not see your high school transcript. What high school decides is which
college you attend and how much it costs — and those do matter.

## What actually matters now

**Your grades and where you land for college.** Not because prestige matters
much for medical school — it matters less than people think — but because
your college has to be a place where you can earn strong grades, get research
or clinical opportunities, and graduate without crushing debt.

**Cost.** Debt from undergrad follows you into a decade of training. A state
school you can afford beats a famous school you cannot.

**Dual credit, if medicine is even a maybe.** This is the one with a
deadline.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The JAMP early route starts in high school">
  Texas runs a program called JAMP that offers economically disadvantaged
  students scholarships, paid summer internships, and guaranteed medical
  school admission. Its **early admission route requires 27 semester hours of
  dual credit completed during your senior year of high school**, with no
  more than 3 AP hours counting. <Source id="jamp-criteria" />

  That is a decision you make now for a program you have probably never heard
  of. Read [the JAMP page](/texas/jamp).
</Callout>

## What to do this semester

- Take the hardest science and math courses you can do well in. "Well" is
  the operative word — a B in AP Biology is not better than an A in regular
  biology if the B came from drowning.
- Look into dual credit at your local community college, especially if money
  is tight.
- Ask one adult who works in healthcare if you can shadow them for a day.
  Anyone: a nurse, a paramedic, a pharmacist. You are not building a resume,
  you are checking whether you like being around sick people.

## What can safely wait

Nearly everything. You do not need research. You do not need hundreds of
volunteer hours. You do not need to know your specialty, and you certainly do
not need to be studying for the MCAT.

<Callout variant="opinion" title="Judgment call">
  High school students who have decided on medicine at fifteen sometimes
  spend four years accumulating credentials for a career they have never
  actually seen up close. Go see the job. If you still want it after a real
  look at a hospital on a bad day, that conviction will carry you further
  than any activity list.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a college purely on name.** Where you go matters much less than
  how you do once you are there.
- **Taking on large undergraduate debt** on the assumption that medicine will
  pay it off later. It might. It will also be joined by medical school debt.
- **Assuming a "pre-med major" is required.** There is no such requirement.
  You can major in anything as long as you complete the prerequisite science
  courses.

## Texas specifics

- Establishing and keeping **Texas residency** is enormously valuable: at
  least 90 percent of seats at Texas public medical schools go to Texas
  residents. If you graduate from a Texas high school, you are likely on the
  strongest residency footing you will ever have. See
  [Texas residency](/texas/residency).
- Going out of state for college can complicate residency. It is not
  disqualifying, but understand the rules before you decide.
